The whole path

Two processes, one database. Ingest owns the feed connection and is the only process that writes. The API never opens the feed: it loads the picture from Mongo once, then follows every change through change streams, and serves the desk from its own copy. A feed outage or an ingest crash leaves the API serving, and several API instances can run side by side.

What the realistic feed does

The realistic feed behaves like a production airport system. Watching it before designing, and again with the anomaly tape once ingest ran, showed each of these, and each has a rule.

One line through ingest

Every line from the feed passes the same gates, in order (feed/framing.ts, then domain/normalize.ts). A line that fails one is kept with the reason in anomalies and shows on the desk's anomaly tape. Nothing rejected reaches the airport picture. On the realistic feed, duplicates are by far the most common reject, then truncated lines, then positions at 0,0.

What applyMessage decides

domain/apply.ts is the single entry point to the rules: one trusted message in, a list of effects out, no I/O. The same function runs in ingest, and its projections (board, mapView, alerts) are what the API and replay read, so live and replay cannot disagree about what the board shows.

What each collection holds

Ingest writes three kinds of data. The feed's own records, kept as received and stamped with the message's ts, seq and connectionEpoch: flights (the version held now), flightVersions (every version) and positions (every report). Records the rules work out, which the feed never sends: alerts, movements and standOccupancy. Logs: events, one row per change for the scrubber and the replay log, and anomalies, every rejected line. meta is a single document describing the feed link.

"One per key" collections are upserts on a unique index, so a message seen twice, or a snapshot repeating a finished flight, changes nothing. "Append" collections only ever grow.

Every row carries the keys that join it to the rest. flightId ties a flight to its versions, its landing or take-off, its stay on a stand, its alerts and its timeline rows. icao24 is the airframe: it joins a flight to that aircraft's positions, and an arrival to its departure. ts is feed time, which is how replay finds the picture at an instant.

How a change reaches the screen

Nothing on the live desk polls for the board, the map or the alerts. Ingest writes to Mongo; the API is told by a change stream, updates its own copy of the airport, and marks which desk topics changed. Twice a second it pushes each changed topic, whole, over one server-sent events connection. The browser swaps that topic in and React re-renders.

A push carries the entire topic (the whole board, the whole map), never a diff. A push that is missed, or a browser that reconnects, is healed by the next one: the desk cannot drift away from the API's copy.

Connections, gaps and restarts

The feed drops whenever it likes. Ingest reconnects immediately, backing off only while connecting fails. Each connection starts again with a hello and a snapshot, and its seq restarts at 1, so every stored record also carries a connectionEpoch. The gap between connections can be seconds (a reconnect) or hours (ingest down), and no rule assumes how long it is.

The only rule that needs to know about the gap is stand occupancy, because the feed has no on-block or off-block times. A change that happened during the gap is only visible in the next snapshot, so only time ingest watched is counted.

One turnaround, every rule

An arrival and its departure share one airframe (reg, icao24). Every rule is phrased in terms of where those two flights are in their lifecycle (domain/flight.ts, domain/stands.ts). An aircraft's flights are ordered by when they land or take off, never by schedule: a late inbound often leaves its departure scheduled before its own arrival.

Feed clock and wall clock

The wall clock is the time on the computer running the code: what Date.now() or new Date() return, the clock on the machine's wall. There are two of them here: the server's (where ingest and the API run) and the browser's (the viewer's laptop).

The feed clock is the ts the airport system stamps on every message: when the feed says the thing happened. A message delivered seconds late still carries the time it was produced. The actual landing and take-off times are on the feed clock too.

They never agree exactly: the newest feed timestamp is typically a second or so ahead of the server's clock, or behind it. The design keeps the airport's history on the feed clock and uses a wall clock only for "now" on the live desk.

With a second of skew none of this shows. It would show if the clocks were far apart: replaying recorded data through ingest (a 10× load test) stamps everything in the past, so a wall-clock "now" would hide every finished flight, and recordings older than six hours would leave the KPI table empty. The fix then is the map's approach everywhere: treat the newest feed time as "now".

Extras, and why

  • Turnarounds on the board. Every row shows the aircraft's registration. Selecting a flight highlights the other half of its turnaround in the other list, and the flight card links to it. Ground staff follow aircraft, not flight numbers, and the stand-conflict rule needs the same pairing.
  • Two alerts and three KPIs. Stand conflicts beside Delayed; on-time performance and stand utilisation beside movements.
  • Replay of the whole desk. Board, map, alerts and KPIs at any instant, with an event-volume scrubber, playback up to 60× and the event log around the cursor. It answers the question ops asks after an incident: what did the board show at 14:32?
  • The anomaly tape. What the normalizer rejected and why, live. It is how the line framer and several alert and movement fixes were found.
  • The wrong-airport lock. A hello for another airport locks the feed out and the desk says so, instead of showing a plausible board for the wrong airport.
  • The truth check. npm run truth-check compares the running desk with the clean feed (tests).
  • A one-command dev stack with hot reload: docker compose up -d.

What is tested, and why

The tests sit where the board's truth is decided. Everything that turns feed messages into flights, alerts and KPIs is pure domain code, so most tests run with no Mongo and no network. Where the logic lives in a Mongo query, the test runs the real query against a throwaway MongoDB. One end-to-end check then compares the running desk with the clean feed.

npm test runs 55 tests: 49 over the domain and 6 against MongoDB 7, the version the stack runs, which mongodb-memory-server downloads once.

The tests can fail. Each store test was checked by breaking, one at a time, the on-time threshold, the hour window, the open-stay filter, the stay's record key, the gap rule, the late-message guard, the restored last-heard time, the anomaly filter and the alert record key, and watching it fail.

The truth check (npm run truth-check) opens its own connection to the clean feed, takes its snapshot as ground truth for 15 seconds, and compares it with what the API serves from the realistic feed: every flight's status, stand and times; the Delayed and stand-conflict alerts; the transmitting aircraft within 15 km and their positions; the landing and take-off log with each time and schedule; and which aircraft are on a stand. It restates the rules itself instead of importing the API's code, so it can catch bugs in them. It exits non-zero on a real mismatch and reports timing separately: messages the realistic feed delivered seconds late, flights the feed retired without a message, aircraft that left coverage moments ago.

Not covered by automated tests: the other Mongo stores (board, replay pictures, timeline), the change-stream mirror and the web UI. The truth check exercises the stores and the mirror against the running stack.

With two more days

  • More analysis of the feed. Record hours of the realistic feed and measure the patterns this design rests on: how late flights run, how long reconnect gaps last, how often stands are reassigned and how often that ends in a conflict, and whether actual times are ever corrected after they are first sent.
  • Review normalization for what still gets through. It checks type, seq and coordinates and trusts the rest. Next: timestamps that parse and are not far in the future, known statuses and kinds, stands that exist in the zones, ARR and DEP consistent with origin and destination, plausible altitude and speed.
  • Settle open questions about movements with the team. Should a landing seen only in the snapshot of a fresh start count? Do go-arounds or diversions count? On-time per hour of landing, or of schedule, and does early count as on time? Should an hour that was only partly watched be marked as partial on the desk?
  • Observability. Structured logs, and metrics for messages per second, rejects by reason, delay from feed time to stored and to pushed, change-stream lag and connected desks. Show feed staleness on the desk (today a dead ingest freezes the board without saying so) and alert when ingest stops hearing the feed.
  • Close the hydrate/watch gap. Start the change streams from the cluster time read before hydrating, so a change landing in between cannot be missed.
  • More code cleanup and clarifications. Build each pushed topic once for all connected desks instead of once per desk. Use one feed-time "now" for the board and the KPIs, as the map already does. Merge the two feed-link types. Keep connectionEpoch across ingest restarts.
  • More tests. Integration tests for the other stores and the mirror on a throwaway replica set; ingest end to end against a fake NDJSON server (framing, reconnects, resume); the web hooks (stale frame requests skipped, one topic swapped per push); property tests for the interval maths.
  • Retention. A TTL or rollups on positions and events, which grow by about ten documents a second.
  • A third alert: runway occupied. Point-in-polygon against the hello zones, tracking who is on the runway as aircraft enter and leave.
  • The 10× load test. Replay recorded NDJSON through ingest at ten times the rate and measure where it bends. The first suspect is that ingest awaits each message's writes in order.

Time and AI usage

Time. About 7 hours in total, AI-assisted throughout (Cursor first, then Claude Code).

Tools. Cursor for the first build: ingest, domain rules, API, the first board, the map and the history scrubber. Claude Code (in VS Code) for the replay rebuild, flightVersions, the Docker dev stack, the desk redesign, a verification pass against the running system, a restructuring with the shared contract package, the on-time and stand-utilisation KPIs and their tests, a naming pass, and these docs. Domain rules were designed and argued over before code was generated, and I can walk through every file.

Where I overrode or redirected the AI

  • Duplicates. The first design dropped anything with seq <= lastSeq. I pointed out that 49 after 50 is a real, new message in this feed, so we moved to a seen-seq set plus per-entity ts freshness.
  • hello as configuration. I treated it as the airport contract, which produced the lock and banner instead of ingesting whatever arrives.
  • Keep what we drop. I asked for the anomaly tape. On real runs it exposed truncated JSON, duplicates and 0,0, which drove the framer and the alert and movement fixes.
  • Wording that matches the system. "In-memory source of truth" became "live working state; Mongo is durable history": the snapshot re-seeds the state, and Mongo remembers.
  • Map behaviour. The first map re-fitted on every update and framed the long approach, so the field looked tiny and the stands seemed missing (all the zones were there). Now it fits the field once and has explicit range buttons. I also rejected "pan when the plane nears the edge": the selected aircraft now stays centred.
  • Layout. The trading-desk ARR | map | DEP layout was my product call.
  • Architecture. Once the base worked, I pushed past "one process is enough" to ingest, change streams and history.
  • Replay as the desk, not a log. The first history screen was an event list; I asked for the live desk at T. When the AI pointed out that flights only keeps the latest version, I asked for a proper flight-version log rather than a workaround, hence flightVersions.
  • Turnarounds. I asked for the registration on every row and for an arrival to highlight its departure, so the same aircraft is easy to follow across the two lists.
  • Purge and re-validate. I wiped Mongo and re-checked after fixes, so the picture was not built on stale data.
  • Parked aircraft. I noticed a boarding aircraft missing from the map while the board showed it on its stand. The AI's "only aircraft heard in the last 30 s" rule could not tell parked from gone. It now keeps silent aircraft stopped on the ground as parked. The same investigation found the inbound aircraft attached to its departure instead of its arrival.
  • Stand minutes against the history. I questioned a stand shown fully occupied for hours in which nothing moved. A stay that ended while ingest was down had been counted until ingest noticed. Now a stay that ends during a gap ends when the feed was last heard.
  • No assumed reconnect rhythm. How often this test feed reconnects is specific to it, not to real feeds, so I had the code checked for any reliance on it: the gap rule works for a gap of any length.
  • Legibility. I asked for full names instead of abbreviations across the code and styles, units of time in one place, and these diagrams.

What AI verification caught

Claude Code checked its own work against the running stack: the live board against a replay frame at the same instant, the stored data against the flight history, logs and process lists. That surfaced these bugs, all but the last two already in the system:

  • alerts that never closed after an ingest restart, or when a flight left the feed;
  • flights that stayed on the live board after the feed dropped them;
  • the wrong-airport lock never reaching the UI;
  • reconnect backoff that never reset, losing up to 30 s of data per drop;
  • a delay alert that reopened while an arrival taxied in;
  • its own Docker setup leaving old ingest processes writing in parallel after dev restarts (the slim image has no ps);
  • its own earlier rule ordering an aircraft's flights by schedule, which mis-paired turnarounds whenever the inbound was late (found while measuring stand overlaps before writing the stand-conflict rule).

Fixes to domain logic come with a test.
